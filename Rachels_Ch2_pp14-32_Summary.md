# Summary: Rachels, *The Elements of Moral Philosophy* (9th ed.)
## Chapter 2 — "The Challenge of Cultural Relativism" (pp. 14–32)

## 2.1 Different Cultures Have Different Moral Codes

Rachels opens with Herodotus's story of Darius, King of Persia. Greeks were horrified at the Callatian custom of eating their dead fathers' bodies; Callatians were equally horrified when asked if they'd burn their dead, as the Greeks did. Each side considered its own custom obviously correct. Rachels extends the point with the Eskimos (Inuit) of the early-to-mid 20th century, whose customs — polygamy and wife-lending, high tolerance for infanticide (especially of female infants), and abandoning elderly relatives in the snow — struck outside observers as shockingly different from Western norms. Since Herodotus, this kind of cross-cultural variation has been well documented: conceptions of right and wrong differ from culture to culture, and assuming everyone shares "our" values is naïve.

## 2.2 Cultural Relativism

This observation — that moral codes vary across societies — leads many people to **Cultural Relativism**: the view that there are no universal moral truths, only the customs of different societies. Sociologist William Graham Sumner put it starkly: "the notion of right is in the folkways... whatever is, is right." Rachels distills the theory into five claims:

1. Different societies have different moral codes.
2. The moral code of a society determines what is right within that society.
3. There is no objective standard that can be used to judge one society's code as better than another's.
4. The moral code of our own society has no special status; it is one among many.
5. It is arrogant to judge other cultures — we should always be tolerant.

Rachels notes a tension: claim 2 (a culture's norms are supreme *within* that culture) can be read as requiring tolerance even of intolerant regimes (e.g., Nazi Germany) — but properly understood, relativism only says a culture's norms bind actions *within its own bounds*, so invading another society (as the Nazis did Poland) violates the invaded society's norms too.

## 2.3 The Cultural Differences Argument

Cultural relativists typically argue as follows:

1. The Greeks believed it was wrong to eat the dead, whereas the Callatians believed it was right. (Or: the Eskimos saw nothing wrong with infanticide, while Americans believe it's immoral.)
2. Therefore, [the practice] is neither objectively right nor wrong — merely a matter of opinion that varies by culture.

Rachels argues this reasoning is **invalid**: the premise concerns what people *believe*, while the conclusion concerns what is *actually* true. From the mere fact that people disagree, it doesn't follow that there's no objective truth — just as disagreement about whether the earth is round doesn't make geography a matter of opinion. The argument doesn't prove its conclusion false, but it fails to establish it either.

## 2.4 What Follows from Cultural Relativism

If Cultural Relativism were true, several uncomfortable consequences follow:

1. **We could no longer call other societies' customs inferior to our own** — not even genuinely harmful practices like China's political repression, since condemning them would require an objective standard relativism denies.
2. **We could no longer criticize our own society's code** — if right and wrong are relative to culture, that applies to our culture too.
3. **The idea of moral progress is undermined** — we couldn't say that, e.g., expanding women's rights was genuine "progress," since judging old norms by new ones requires exactly the kind of transcultural standard relativism forbids.

Because most people firmly believe practices like slavery are wrong everywhere, and that its abolition was real progress, these consequences count heavily against Cultural Relativism.

## 2.5 Why There Is Less Disagreement Than There Seems to Be

Much apparent moral disagreement is actually disagreement in *factual or religious belief*, not values. Example: a culture that refuses to eat cows because it believes human souls (e.g., a grandmother's) may inhabit them doesn't have different values from ours — we both agree one shouldn't eat one's grandmother; we merely disagree about whether the cow might *be* Grandma. Customs are shaped by many factors (values, beliefs, environment), so differing customs don't automatically mean differing values.

## 2.6 Some Values Are Shared by All Cultures

Rachels argues certain values are logically necessary for any enduring society, and so appear in every culture:

- **Caring for children** — a society that didn't protect its young would die out.
- **Truthfulness** — without a presumption of honesty, communication (and thus society) breaks down.
- **A prohibition against murder** — without it, no large-scale society could function safely.

Applying the Eskimo case: their apparent tolerance of infanticide wasn't a rejection of the value of children's lives but a last resort forced by extreme scarcity, nomadism, and long nursing periods — Eskimo values were "much like our own," just facing harsher circumstances.

## 2.7 Judging a Cultural Practice to Be Undesirable

Using the real case of **Fauziya Kassindja**, a 17-year-old from Togo who fled to the U.S. in 1996 to escape "excision" (female genital mutilation) and was then imprisoned for 18 months while seeking asylum, Rachels asks whether we can legitimately call excision wrong — or whether that's just imposing our own cultural standard.

He proposes a **culture-independent standard**: *does a practice promote or hinder the welfare of the people affected by it?* Excision causes severe physical harm (pain, permanent loss of sexual pleasure, infection, sometimes death) with no clear social benefit (unlike, say, Eskimo infanticide, which served group survival). Arguments offered in its defense (curbing promiscuity, increasing marital fidelity) don't hold up well under scrutiny.

Rachels also examines why thoughtful people hesitate to criticize other cultures: guilt over colonial history, a general commitment to tolerance, and reluctance to seem contemptuous. He argues none of these justify silence — judging a specific practice as wrong is not the same as condemning an entire culture or endorsing military intervention; tolerance doesn't require regarding all practices as equally good.

## 2.8 Back to the Five Claims

Rachels revisits the five relativist claims in light of the discussion:

1. **True, but overstated** — cultures do differ, but some values (truthfulness, caring for children, anti-murder norms) are shared, and apparent differences often stem from differing factual beliefs rather than differing values.
2–3. **False** — a society's code is not infallible. Examples: a Nigerian woman sentenced to death by stoning for extramarital sex (2002); a Saudi gang-rape victim sentenced to lashes (2007). Societies, like individuals, can be morally wrong and in need of improvement — this requires a standard not tied to any one culture (Rachels again suggests: whether a practice helps or harms those affected).
4. **Partly true** — our society's code has no automatic special status, but that doesn't mean all moral codes are equally good; ours could be relatively better or worse.
5. **Overstated** — tolerance is a virtue, but not everything deserves toleration; tolerating torture, slavery, or rape would not be virtuous.

## 2.9 What We Can Learn from Cultural Relativism

Although Rachels rejects the theory (unsound argument, implausible consequences, exaggerated disagreement), he credits it with two genuine insights:

1. **Not all our practices rest on absolute rational necessity.** Many are merely conventional, and it's easy to forget this. Example: monogamous marriage is often treated as the only legitimate way to pursue romantic happiness, but alternatives (consensual open relationships, polyamory) aren't necessarily immoral — they may just be different ways of organizing intimate life, provided they're built on honesty and consent.
2. **Relativism can guard against dogmatism and prompt open-mindedness.** As Herodotus observed, people naturally think their own customs and beliefs are best. Recognizing this can help us notice when our strong reactions (e.g., discomfort about homosexuality) are products of cultural conditioning rather than genuine moral insight, making us more receptive to reexamining inherited views.

**Overall takeaway:** Cultural Relativism starts from a true and important observation — moral codes vary across societies — but overreaches by concluding that there is no objective morality at all. Rachels argues we can accept the theory's genuine insights (humility, openness, recognition of convention) while rejecting its central claim that nothing can be judged right or wrong across cultures.
